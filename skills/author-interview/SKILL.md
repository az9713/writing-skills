# Author Interview

The author may have never written anything. Assume nothing: no topic, no notes, no stories written down, no writing samples, no knowledge of book terms. The interview turns what the author says into material the book-writing skill can use.

## Rules for every mode

1. Ask **one question at a time**. Wait for the answer.
2. Use plain words. Never use book-trade or skill terms (thesis, archetype, persona, R0/R1, Class A/B/C). Say "the main idea", "the reader", "how you write".
3. Accept any answer form: typed, dictated, bullet points, bad spelling, half sentences. Tell the author this at the start.
4. **Save the author's words exactly as given.** Fix nothing before saving. The raw words are the evidence and the voice sample.
5. Never invent a fact, a story, a feeling, a name, a number, or a credential. When an answer is vague, ask a follow-up question. Do not fill the gap.
6. Offer a way out of every question: "Skip", "I don't know", or "Give me an example answer". An example answer is labelled as an example and is never saved as the author's answer.
7. Keep each session short: 6–8 questions, then stop and show what was collected.
8. Save each session to `book/interviews/YYYY-MM-DD-<mode>.md` (create the folder if it does not exist). Format:

```markdown
# Interview — <mode> — YYYY-MM-DD
Source: author's own words (typed | dictated)

## Q1. <question as asked>
<answer exactly as given>

## Summary (written by the AI, not the author's words)
- ...
```

The summary is Class C (the AI's reasoning). The answers are Class A (supplied by the author). Keep them apart.

## Mode: topic — "I don't know what to write about"

Goal: 2–3 candidate topics that the author can write about honestly.

Ask about, in this order (adapt the wording):

1. What do people ask you for help with?
2. What have you done for years — job, hobby, caring for someone, a sport, a craft?
3. What hard problem have you solved in your own life?
4. What do you know now that you wish you had known 5 years ago?
5. What could you talk about for an hour without notes?
6. Who would you most like to help? Describe one real person.
7. Is this book for fun, for money, for your business, or for your family?

Then list 2–3 candidate topics. For each, state: the reader, the problem the book solves, and **what the author has that makes them able to write it** (quote their answer). Mark a topic `WEAK BASIS` when the author's answers show little experience of it, and say why.

The author chooses. Do not choose for them.

## Mode: knowledge — "I have no notes or sources"

Goal: source notes for Phase B, in the author's words.

For the chosen topic, ask:

1. Walk me through how you do <the thing>, step by step, as if I'm watching.
2. What do beginners get wrong?
3. What did you get wrong at first?
4. What tools, numbers, or times matter? (for example: temperatures, amounts, hours)
5. What do other people or other books say that you disagree with? Why?
6. What is the one thing a reader must remember?

Repeat across several sessions, one chapter area at a time. Record each saved file in the book's source ledger as `type: author interview`.

Flag every factual statement in the answers (numbers, health, safety, law, money) as `needs checking`. The author's experience is evidence of what happened to them. It is not proof of a general fact.

## Mode: story — filling an [AUTHOR EXPERIENCE NEEDED HERE] marker

Goal: the facts for one real story, then a draft the author approves.

1. Tell the author what the story is for, in one sentence: "This section needs a time when <X> happened to you."
2. Ask: When was it? Where were you? What happened first? What went wrong or surprised you? What did you do? How did it end? What did you learn?
3. Ask what they felt or thought at the key moment — only if they want to share it.
4. Write the story as a paragraph using **only** the facts given. Keep the author's own phrases where they work.
5. Show the draft next to the answers. Mark any sentence that adds a detail not in the answers, and remove it unless the author confirms it.
6. The author approves, edits, or rejects. Only an approved story goes into the manuscript.

If the author has no such story, say so plainly and offer the honest options: a different story they do have, a story from someone else with that person's permission, or a clearly labelled imagined example ("Imagine a nurse who…").

## Mode: voice — "I have no writing samples"

Goal: a writing sample for book-writing §5.1, made of the author's own words.

1. Ask 6–8 open questions on the book's topic that invite a longer answer, for example: "Tell me about the first time this went badly." "Explain <the thing> to a friend who has never tried it." "What annoys you about how others teach this?"
2. Ask the author to answer as they would talk. Dictation gives the best sample.
3. Save the answers unedited. That file is the sample.
4. Hand back to book-writing §5.1 with `Voice Source: interview`.

If the author will not or cannot answer at length, fall back to book-writing §5.2 method 3 (choose by reaction).

---

## Handing back

End every session with:

- what was saved, and the file path;
- what is still missing;
- the one sentence the author can type to continue, for example "Continue my book" or "Interview me about chapter 3".
